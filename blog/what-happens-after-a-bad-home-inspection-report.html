What Happens After a Bad Home Inspection Report? Your Options in Kentucky

July 23, 2026 · 2 min read

A scary-looking inspection report is one of the most stressful moments in a home purchase — but it's rarely the end of the deal. In Kentucky, if you're within your inspection contingency period, you have several paths forward. Here's how to think about them.

First, separate the report into tiers

Every inspection report contains a mix of items. Sort them: safety and major-system defects (roof at end of life, failed HVAC, active leaks, electrical hazards, structural movement, a bad sewer lateral), meaningful but routine repairs (water heater near end of life, minor roof repair, grading), and cosmetic or maintenance items (caulk, a stuck window, a missing outlet cover). Most of a long report is the third category. Focus your negotiation on the first.

Option 1: Ask the seller to make repairs

You can submit a written request for the seller to repair specific items before closing. The upside is the work gets done without you managing it; the downside is you don't control the contractor or the quality, and repairs done under deadline pressure are sometimes minimal. Reserve repair requests for items where doing it right matters — roofing, electrical, plumbing, HVAC.

Option 2: Ask for a price reduction or closing credit

Often the cleaner path: the seller credits you money at closing (or reduces the price) and you handle the repairs yourself afterward, with your own contractor and your own standards. Lenders cap how large a seller credit can be relative to the purchase price, so very large concessions sometimes have to be split between a price cut and a credit. This is the most common resolution in the Louisville market.

Option 3: Renegotiate, re-inspect, or walk

For a major surprise — a failed foundation, a roof that needs full replacement, widespread polybutylene — you can reopen the overall deal, bring in a specialist for a focused re-inspection to scope the true cost, or, if you're within your contingency, terminate the contract and recover your earnest money per the contract terms. Walking away from a home with a five-figure hidden problem is a valid, sometimes correct, outcome.

Keep perspective

Nearly every resale home in Louisville — especially anything built before 1980 — will generate a report with real findings. A thorough inspector's job is to document everything, which makes even a sound house look daunting on paper. Ask the inspector directly which items they'd prioritize if it were their purchase; a good one will tell you plainly. For how the back-and-forth actually works, see our guide to negotiating repairs after an inspection in Kentucky.

Key takeaways

  • Sort the report into safety/major-system defects, routine repairs, and cosmetic items — negotiate on the first tier.
  • Repair requests get the work done but you don't control quality; credits let you use your own contractor.
  • Closing credits and price reductions are the most common resolution in the Louisville market, subject to lender caps.
  • For a major hidden defect, re-inspecting with a specialist or terminating within your contingency are legitimate options.
